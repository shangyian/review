Replication and Consistency

When data has more than one copy, every read and every failure forces a choice about what a reader may see. This page goes through the failures in the order they tend to bite. Three of the screens are live labs.

1Why keep copies

The problem

One copy of the data is one machine to lose, one machine to read from, and one place that may be far from some users.

The fix

Keep copies. The common setup, used through the rest of this page, has one leader that takes every write and ships a log of changes to replicas. Replicas can survive a failure, answer reads, and sit near users. PostgreSQL's streaming replication, for example, is asynchronous by default, with a delay that is typically under one second if the standby can keep up. source

The price

A copy is only as current as the log it has received. For a moment after a write, the leader and the replicas disagree. Every failure on the next screens comes from that gap, and the choices to close it cost latency, availability, or both.

2A replica shows old data

The problem

A user saves a change, reloads, and sees the old version, because the read went to a replica that has not received the write yet. Or they see new data, reload, and see older data, because the second read hit a replica that is further behind.

What it tells you

In the lab, each replica trails the leader by between half and one and a half times the average lag. The chart shows how likely a read is to be stale at each moment after the write. With any replica, nearly every read in the first moments is stale. The lag in real systems varies with load. The PostgreSQL docs describe the usual delay as under a second and note it can grow if the standby cannot keep up. source

The fix

  • Read recent writes from the leader. For a short window after a user writes, send that user's reads to the leader. It costs leader load, and you must choose the window.
  • Keep each user on one replica. The same replica can still be behind, but it can't go backward, so the user never sees time run in reverse.
  • Wait until the replica has caught up. The read, or the write, waits for the replica to apply the change. PostgreSQL's remote_apply setting makes a commit wait until the standby has replayed it, which the docs say allows load balancing with causal consistency. source

Some stores let you choose per read. DynamoDB reads are eventually consistent by default, and a ConsistentRead option returns the latest data, at twice the cost. Strong reads are not available on global secondary indexes or streams. source

The price

Fresh reads cost leader load or waiting time. Else, latency against consistency (PACELC).

3A failover loses writes

The problem

The leader crashes. A replica takes over, but the replica only has the writes that had already arrived. Everything the old leader had acknowledged to clients and not yet shipped is gone. PostgreSQL's docs say so directly: with asynchronous shipping there is a window for data loss, and the amount lost is proportional to the replication delay at the time of failover. source

What it tells you

Each tick is a write that was acknowledged to a client. Blue ticks reached the replica before the crash and orange ones did not. The number lost is the write rate times the lag. Doubling either one doubles the loss. An old leader that comes back may also hold writes the new leader never saw, so the two histories have to be reconciled or one thrown away.

The fix

Make the leader wait. In synchronous replication, each commit waits until it is confirmed on disk on both the primary and the standby, and data is lost only if both crash at once. source Tick "that replica is down" with synchronous replication.

The price

Every write now waits at least a round trip to the replica. And if the synchronous standby fails, the docs say commits may never complete. The mitigation is to list several candidate standbys, so another can take over, or to reduce the number required. source Else, latency against consistency, and in a failure, availability against consistency.

4Split brain

The problem

A network partition cuts the cluster in two. The old leader is on one side and cannot reach the others. The other side decides the leader is gone and elects a new one. Now two nodes both believe they lead, and both accept writes.

What you see

Step through five nodes: a healthy cluster, then a partition, then an election on the larger side, then a write on each side, and finally the partition heals. Switch the rule to see what a majority requirement changes.

The fix

  • Require a majority. In Raft, a log entry is committed once the leader has replicated it on a majority of the servers, and a new leader needs votes from a majority. A leader cut off from the majority cannot commit anything, and a server that sees a higher term reverts to follower. source
  • Fence the old leader. A paused or cut-off node may not know it lost its lease. Give each new leader a larger number, make the storage layer remember the highest number it has seen, and refuse writes with a smaller one. Kleppmann notes a pause can hit at any point, even right after a check, so checking the lease before writing is not enough. source

The price

The smaller side stops accepting writes, and with fewer than a majority of nodes up, nobody does. During a partition, consistency over availability (PACELC).

5Quorums

The problem

Some stores have no single leader. A write goes to every copy and the store returns once enough of them confirm, and a read asks several copies. How many is enough is a dial, and it trades freshness against latency and availability.

copies that are down:

What it tells you

With N copies, a write waits for W confirmations and a read asks R copies. If R + W is greater than N, the copies a read asks must include at least one that has the write. The Cassandra docs state it as W + R > RF, and give QUORUM as a majority of the replicas. source In the lab the three copies answer in 3, 5 and 40 ms. Waiting for the slow one costs far more than waiting for the fast one.

Dynamo reports a common configuration of N=3, R=2, W=2, and notes that raising W shrinks the window where a write is not yet durable, but may reject more requests because more hosts must be alive. source

The price

A bigger W or R is fresher and slower, and fails when too many copies are down. A smaller one is fast and available, and can return stale data. Dynamo relaxes this further with a sloppy quorum and hinted handoff, so a write can land on other nodes while a copy is down. That keeps writes available at the cost of weaker freshness. source Else, latency against consistency. During a failure, availability against consistency.

6Writes in two places at once

The problem

Two regions each accept writes to the same item, say a shopping cart. The writes cross in flight, and each region must decide what the cart now contains.

What it tells you

Region A adds a book. 200 ms later, region B adds a pen. With last writer wins, the newer timestamp survives and the other write disappears. But timestamps come from clocks. If region B's clock runs more than 200 ms behind, the pen is stamped as older and the later write is the one discarded. Cassandra resolves conflicts this way, by timestamp, and its docs recommend running a process such as NTP to keep clocks close. source

The fix

  • Merge. Keep both changes. Dynamo's own example is the shopping cart, where an add-to-cart operation should never be lost, so conflicting versions are merged and the cart keeps both items. It uses vector clocks to tell which versions truly conflict. source
  • One home region per item. Send every write for a cart to one region, which puts them in a single order. Other regions forward their writes there.

The price

Merging needs logic that fits the data: a union works for a cart and not for a bank balance. A home region makes distant writes pay a cross-region trip, and the item is unavailable for writes if its home region is unreachable.

7The ladder, and choosing per operation

What you see

Consistency is a ladder of guarantees, not one setting. Jepsen's map of consistency models orders them by implication: strict serializable implies linearizable, which implies sequential, then causal, then the per-session guarantees such as read your writes and monotonic reads. It also notes that weaker models can be totally available, that read your writes and stronger can be at most sticky available, and that sequential and stronger cannot be totally available. source The plain definitions in the figure are mine.

Choose per operation, not per system

  • Moving money, picking the one scheduler: the top of the ladder. Pay the latency and accept that the minority side stops.
  • Showing a user their own edit: read your writes is enough. Read from the leader or a caught-up replica.
  • A feed, a counter, a recommendation: eventual is usually fine, and it is the fastest and the most available.

The PACELC summary of the screens above: during a partition a store favors availability or consistency (screens 4 and 5). Otherwise it favors latency or consistency (screens 2, 3 and 5). The right setting differs by operation, so a good design lets you choose per read or per write, as DynamoDB's ConsistentRead flag and Cassandra's consistency levels do.

The simulations use round numbers I picked: replication lags, a 5 ms round trip, and replica response times of 3, 5 and 40 ms. The plain-language definitions on screen 7 and the idea that a returning old leader may hold unreplicated writes are general knowledge, not quoted from the sources.
