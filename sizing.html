Sizing

How big does a system need to be? Sizing is a short method, applied to every resource, and checked two ways. The drawing is a live calculator, and the last screen sizes a real job cluster.

Input: rate and latency

1Rate × latency = requests in flight

The method

Two inputs set how many requests are in the system at once: how fast work arrives (the rate), and how long each one takes from start to finish (the latency). Multiply them, then multiply by what each one uses. This is Little's law: the average number in a system equals the arrival rate times the average time each item spends in it. It holds for any system you draw a boundary around, and for any arrival pattern, as long as the system is steady. source

What you see

Each bar is a job running over a 3-hour slice, and the dashed line marks one moment. The jobs the line crosses are the jobs in flight, which matches rate times latency. Multiply by GPUs per job and you have the GPUs busy on average. The units must cancel: jobs per hour times hours leaves a plain count.

Input: size

2Size: bits versus bytes

The method

Size is the bytes each request carries or stores. It sets network and storage, and the common slips are in the units.

Traps

  • Bits and bytes. A link quoted in Gbps counts bits, and there are 8 bits in a byte. Gbps to GB/s means dividing by 8.
  • Prefixes. A bigger unit means a smaller number: 1,000,000 KB is 1,000 MB.
  • Time units. A rate gets smaller when the time unit gets smaller. 30 per hour is 0.5 per minute, not 1,800.
  • Dropped digits. Slips of a factor of 10 are the most common.

Checks

Do the estimate two ways. Rate × time × size gives GPUs busy, and total work ÷ the window should match, as the drawing shows with the screen 1 numbers. When two routes disagree, one has a slip. Then ask whether the answer is plausible, and treat anything within a factor of 2 as a good estimate. When the number matters, replace assumptions with a load test.

Input: retention

3Retention: storage grows until data expires

The method

Every request that writes data adds to storage, and storage stops growing only when old data is deleted. Space needed = rate × size × retention, so doubling the retention doubles the disks. Load grows too, so also size for next year's rate.

What you see

The solid line is the data kept today. It climbs until the retention window is full, then flattens, because new data replaces what expires. The dashed line adds a year of growth in the rate. The readouts turn each into disks, at the same 4 TB machines and utilization target as the resource screen.

Resources

4Which resource runs out first

The method

A request uses compute, memory, storage, network, and connections, and each runs out at a different point. Work out how many machines each resource needs, then buy for the largest. Google's SRE book makes a related point: measure capacity in resources such as CPU cores, not in requests per second, because the cost of a request varies and shifts over time. source

What you see

Each bar is the number of machines that one resource needs. The machine is 8 vCPU, 32 GB, 10 Gbps, a 4 TB disk and 5,000 connections, run at your target utilization. The highest bar decides the fleet size, and the preset buttons move the answer between the five. Memory and connections depend on the latency (requests in flight = rate × latency), which is why a slow request is expensive even when it uses little CPU.

Target: latency goal

5Latency goal: why not run at 100%

The method

You pay for the peak, not the average, and you don't run flat out at the peak. Two adjustments: multiply the average by how much higher the peak is, and divide by a target utilization below 100%.

Why not 100%

The curve uses the simplest queueing model, one server with random arrivals and random service times. There the average time in the system is the service time divided by (1 − utilization). source At 50% utilization that is 2 times the service time, at 70% about 3.3 times, at 90% 10 times, and as utilization approaches 100% it grows without limit. Real systems differ from this model, but the shape is the same: delay climbs steeply near full. That is why headroom is worth paying for.

The bars below the curve show average, peak, and the capacity you must provision to hold your target utilization at the peak.

Target: availability

6Availability: surviving a lost zone

The method

Capacity you need on a good day is not capacity you need on a bad one. If you spread across Z zones and want to survive losing one, the remaining Z − 1 zones must carry everything, so the total grows by Z ÷ (Z − 1). With 3 zones that is 1.5 times, with 2 zones it is 2 times, with 6 zones 1.2 times.

What you see

Each column is a zone. The outline is the capacity you bought, and the fill is the load. Without the extra capacity, losing a zone pushes the others past 100%, shown in orange. More zones make survival cheaper, but each added zone also adds operating cost and cross-zone traffic.

Big jobs add a second problem. A job that needs 100 GPUs at once needs 100 free GPUs, usually on connected machines, so a cluster can have idle GPUs and still be unable to start it. Leave extra room for the largest job.

Case: batch work

7Batch work: size from the deadline

The method

For batch work, nobody needs the answer instantly, so size from the total. Add up the work, divide by the time allowed, and that is the capacity busy on average. The overnight jobs here are 500 jobs of 8 GPUs for 30 minutes, plus 30 monster jobs of 100 GPUs for 3 hours.

What you see

Total work is 2,000 + 9,000 = 11,000 GPU-hours. Divide by the deadline to get GPUs busy, then by utilization to get GPUs to provision. Finishing by morning needs about ten times what a four-day deadline needs. A longer deadline lowers the cluster size in direct proportion, so the question to ask is how long the work may wait.

Case

8The job cluster

The requirements

  • Daytime: 300 jobs over 12 hours, each 15 to 30 minutes, each using 1 to 4 GPUs.
  • Overnight: about 500 jobs of 8 GPUs for 30 minutes, and about 30 monster jobs of 100 GPUs for 3 hours.

The estimate

Daytime. 300 jobs over 12 hours is 25 jobs per hour. At 15 to 30 minutes that is 6 to 12.5 jobs in flight, and at 1 to 4 GPUs each, between 6 and 50 GPUs busy, about 23 with middle values. Daytime work totals only about 281 GPU-hours.

Overnight. 500 × 8 × 0.5 = 2,000 GPU-hours of regular jobs, and 30 × 100 × 3 = 9,000 GPU-hours of monsters. The monsters are over 80% of the work. Divide by the deadline: finishing in 12 hours takes about 917 GPUs busy, and in 24 hours about 458. Divide by the utilization target for what to buy, and multiply by the zone factor if you must survive a zone loss.

What to decide. The monsters decide everything, so the useful questions are whether they have a deadline or can take days, whether they can be spread across nights, and whether a 100-GPU, 3-hour job can run on 50 GPUs for 6 hours. If the work scales that way, the GPU-hours stay the same but the peak need halves. Whatever you buy, a 100-GPU job needs 100 GPUs free together.

The job sizes and counts are example numbers. The utilization curve is the simple one-server queueing model, so it overstates or understates real systems. The machine in the screen 4 calculator (8 vCPU, 32 GB, 10 Gbps, 4 TB, 5,000 connections) and the memory and CPU per request are illustrative.
